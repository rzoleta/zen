Optimal Japanese vocabulary learning with Anki

A self-contained setup guide and curriculum, based on the current consensus among the immersion-learning community (TheMoeWay, Tatsumoto, Refold, the Kaishi maintainers, and the FSRS developers). Last researched September 2026.

The method in one paragraph

You learn vocabulary in Anki and everything else from native content. Anki gets you to the point where immersion stops being a wall of noise; immersion does the actual teaching. Concretely: learn kana first, then grind a premade deck of the 1,500 most useful words (Kaishi 1.5k) at 10 to 20 new words a day while skimming one grammar guide and watching or reading things you enjoy. Once the premade deck runs out, you switch to "mining": pulling new words out of your own immersion and turning them into Anki cards with one or two clicks. Anki stays a 20 to 40 minute daily chore. Immersion gets the rest of your time.

None of this is exotic. Spaced repetition and retrieval practice are two of the best-replicated effects in memory research. The community's contribution is the tooling and the specific settings, which is what the rest of this document covers.

Stage 0: kana (1 to 3 weeks)

Learn hiragana and katakana before touching a vocabulary deck. Don't use Anki for this; recognition drilling sites like kana.pro or a couple of days with a chart plus reading practice get you there faster than a months-long SRS deck for 92 characters. You'll be slow at reading kana for a while. That's fine. Kaishi has furigana and audio, so the deck itself will keep training your kana speed.

Stage 1: install and configure Anki

Install

  1. Anki desktop from apps.ankiweb.net. The desktop app is required for add-ons and mining; AnkiDroid (Android) or AnkiMobile (iOS) sync with it for reviewing on the go.
  2. The AnkiConnect add-on (code 2055492159). Every mining tool talks to Anki through it. You don't need it on day one, but install it now so the setup is done.

Turn on FSRS

Anki ships with two scheduling algorithms: the old SM-2 derivative and FSRS. Use FSRS. This is not a close call. FSRS fits its schedule to your actual review history, and in the developers' benchmarks it predicts recall better than SM-2, which translates to fewer reviews for the same retention. It also eliminates "ease hell," the old SM-2 failure mode where lapsed cards got stuck at short intervals forever. Older guides (Tatsumoto's among them) contain elaborate ease-factor workarounds like 131% starting ease. All of that is legacy advice. FSRS makes it unnecessary.

Enable it once, globally: deck options → scroll to the FSRS section → toggle it on. Anki 23.10 or later has it built in.

Grading: two buttons, not four

Anki offers Again, Hard, Good, and Easy. Use only Again (you forgot) and Good (you remembered). This is the item on which the community is most unanimous, and the FSRS developers explicitly endorse two-button use.

The reason is that Hard is a trap. Hard means "I recalled it, barely." It is a pass, and FSRS schedules it as one. People instinctively press Hard when they half-forgot a word, which tells the algorithm the opposite of the truth and quietly corrupts the schedule of every card they do it to. Easy is less dangerous but adds a judgment call to every single review. Four-way grading forces you to spend attention deciding what button to press when you should be spending it on the word. Binary grading is one honest question: did you know it or not?

You can simply ignore the extra buttons, or install the Pass/Fail 2 add-on to remove them so the temptation is gone.

What counts as a pass: you produced the word's reading and knew its meaning before flipping the card. Both, not either. Guessed the meaning but blanked on the reading? That's an Again. Be strict; lenient grading feels productive and teaches you nothing.

Stage 2: the starter deck, Kaishi 1.5k

Download Kaishi 1.5k from AnkiWeb and start it the day you finish kana. It is the community's current answer to "which premade deck," and it earned the spot: 1,500 words chosen by frequency analysis across multiple corpora, every card with a curated example sentence, native word and sentence audio, pitch accent graphs, and modern templates that look fine on a phone.

It displaced the older recommendations for concrete reasons. Core 2k/6k has mistranslations and mismatched pictures the maintainers never fixed. Tango N5/N4 wastes cards on things like country names and ナンプラー (fish sauce). Kaishi is the same idea with a decade of accumulated complaints actually addressed; its maintainers fixed around 120 of the 1,500 inherited sentences during curation.

What a card looks like

結ぶ
靴のひもを結ぶ。
結ぶ（むすぶ）
to tie, to bind
I tie my shoelaces.  🔊 word audio  🔊 sentence audio  + pitch accent graph

Front: the word, with its source sentence in smaller type below and the target word highlighted. Back: reading, meaning, sentence translation, audio, pitch accent. This "word card with sentence context" format is the one to copy for your own cards later. The community argued for years about word cards versus sentence cards (whole sentence on the front); the rough resolution is that pure sentence cards are slow to review and let you memorize the sentence instead of the word, while pure isolated words lose the context that ambiguous words need. Word on front, sentence available but de-emphasized, is the practical middle and what Kaishi ships.

One direction only: Japanese on the front, meaning on the back. No production cards (English → Japanese). Recognition is what immersion demands, production emerges from output practice later, and reverse cards double your review load for little gain.

What about kanji study? Skip it. The old advice was to complete Remembering the Kanji (2,000+ isolated characters with mnemonic stories) before learning any words. The current consensus is that this front-loads months of effort with no comprehension payoff, and that you learn kanji fine as components of the words you're already studying. 結 is not a thing you study; it's the shape that 結ぶ and 結婚 share. The one exception: if you genuinely cannot tell similar kanji apart, a short radical/component deck is a reasonable supplement. Full RTK is not.

Stage 3: grammar, outside Anki

Grammar gets read, not memorized. Pick one guide, read it faster than feels responsible, and let immersion make it stick. You are building a rough map so that when you meet a structure in the wild you half-recognize it; you are not preparing for a grammar exam. A pass through a beginner guide should take weeks, not months, and it runs concurrently with Kaishi, a couple of grammar points a day.

  • Yokubi: the current top recommendation on TheMoeWay; concise and written for this exact method.
  • Tae Kim's Guide to Japanese Grammar: the old standard, still perfectly good. Free, comprehensive, slightly dated in places.
  • Cure Dolly's "Japanese from Scratch" (YouTube) or Japanese Ammo with Misa: if you'd rather watch than read. Cure Dolly's model of the language is genuinely clarifying if you can get past the synthesized voice.

Some people put grammar points into Anki anyway. It's not forbidden, but the community treats it as optional at best; grammar is pattern recognition, and patterns come from seeing thousands of real sentences, not from a card that says "〜ながら = while."

Stage 4: immersion, from day one

Immersion is not the reward for finishing Anki; it's the other half of the method and it starts immediately, even when you understand almost nothing. Watch and read things you would enjoy in English. Tolerating ambiguity is the actual skill being trained in the early months.

  • Beginner-stage balance leans listening-heavy (TheMoeWay suggests roughly 7:3 listening to reading), shifting toward reading as vocabulary grows.
  • Active immersion: full attention, Japanese subtitles if watching, look things up when curiosity strikes but don't pause on every unknown.
  • Passive listening: rewatched shows or podcasts in your ears while commuting or doing chores. Free extra hours.
  • Target: 1 to 2 hours daily minimum. Anki alone, without input, produces someone who knows 1,500 flashcards and no Japanese.

The lookup tool: Yomitan

Install Yomitan (the maintained fork of Yomichan) in your browser. Hover over any Japanese word while holding Shift and it shows reading, meaning, pitch accent, and frequency. This one extension is what makes reading native content viable years before you'd otherwise be ready, and via AnkiConnect it's also your card creator: a click on the + icon in its popup makes a properly formatted Anki card.

Set it up once, properly: import a bilingual dictionary (Jitendex/JMdict), a frequency dictionary (e.g. JPDB), and a pitch accent dictionary, and point its Anki integration at your mining deck. The guides at donkuri's setup page and learnjapanese.moe walk through it, including a local audio server add-on so every mined card gets native audio automatically.

Stage 5: mining your own cards

Somewhere past the halfway point of Kaishi, when immersion starts yielding sentences where you know every word but one, begin mining. Once Kaishi is finished, mining is your only source of new cards, forever. This is the payoff of the whole setup: your deck stops being someone else's frequency list and becomes a record of your own reading and watching, which is both better targeted and far more memorable. You remember where you met a word.

The rule for what to mine is Refold's "1T" heuristic: sentences with exactly one unknown thing. Card format: same as Kaishi. Word on front, the sentence you found it in below, reading/meaning/audio/screenshot on the back. The tools fill in everything automatically; a mined card should cost you two clicks, not two minutes.

Where Migaku fits. Migaku is a paid subscription that bundles this whole stack: popup dictionary, Netflix/YouTube mining, and its own SRS, in one polished package. It works, and reviewers consistently rate it the smoothest option. The enthusiast community still mostly runs the free stack (Yomitan + asbplayer + AnkiConnect) because it costs nothing, keeps your cards in Anki under your control, and is more customizable. Treat Migaku as the convenience alternative, not the standard. The standard is free.

The daily routine

  1. Anki reviews, to zero, every day. No exceptions; a skipped day compounds. If you're pressed for time, do reviews and skip new cards.
  2. 10 to 20 new cards (Kaishi at first, mined cards later).
  3. A grammar point or two while you're still in the beginner guide.
  4. 1 to 2+ hours of immersion. This is the bulk of the time budget, and it should stay that way. If Anki regularly exceeds 30 to 40 minutes, lower new cards or desired retention; never fix it by cutting immersion.
